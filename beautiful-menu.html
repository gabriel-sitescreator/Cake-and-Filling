Bakery Menu Design: How to Create a Beautiful Menu for a Cake or Dessert Business

A guide for bakery owners, cake shop operators, and dessert businesses.

The design of a bakery menu represents one of the most critical touchpoints between a confectionery brand and its customers. Far beyond serving as a simple list of products and prices, a well-engineered menu operates as a silent salesperson, a brand ambassador, and a strategic tool for maximizing commercial profitability. For bakery owners, cake shop operators, and dessert businesses, creating a functional menu requires balancing aesthetic appeal with psychological pricing, visual hierarchy, and precise typographic readability. The subsequent analysis explores the intersection of commercial strategy and graphic design, detailing how confectionery businesses can build menus that drive sales, elevate brand perception, and provide a seamless customer experience across physical and digital platforms.

1. What a Good Bakery Menu Should Communicate

A successful bakery menu operates on the foundational principle of reducing cognitive load for the consumer. When customers encounter a menu, they are attempting to quickly decipher the scope of the business's offerings. A professionally designed menu must instantly communicate what products are available, the overarching categories the business offers, clear product names, concise descriptions, exact prices or price ranges, and relevant variations such as sizes, portions, or flavors. Furthermore, it must outline important customization options, contact information, ordering instructions, and essential terms of service, such as minimum order quantities and advance notice requirements.

The primary objective is to answer the customer's most pressing questions without inducing information overload. When a menu becomes excessively cluttered with paragraphs of text or complex ordering rules, the customer's decision-making process slows down, often leading to decision fatigue or cart abandonment in digital settings. There is a distinct operational difference between essential menu information and supplementary details. While a menu must list the starting price and flavor profile of a custom celebration cake, the granular details of the cake's internal structural supports, the exact brand of chocolate utilized, or the comprehensive allergen matrix can be reserved for a dedicated product page, a website FAQ, or a direct WhatsApp consultation. A well-engineered menu filters out the noise, presenting only the data necessary to guide the customer toward a purchase decision efficiently.

2. How to Organize Bakery Products

Logical categorization forms the architectural foundation of any effective bakery menu. Products must be grouped in a manner that reflects how customers naturally browse and shop for desserts. When every product is presented at the same visual level in a single uninterrupted list, the menu becomes an impenetrable wall of text.

To prevent this, products should be divided into distinct, intuitive categories. Examples of effective categorization include grouping items under headings such as "Custom Cakes," "Celebration Cakes," "Mini Cakes," "Slices," "Cupcakes," "Cheesecakes," "Tarts and Pies," "Cookies and Brownies," "Seasonal Desserts," and "Gift Boxes." If the establishment operates as a café, "Drinks and Coffee" should form a distinct section. These divisions allow a customer searching for a casual afternoon pastry to bypass the high-end wedding cake section entirely. Handling a large product catalog requires strategic visual hierarchy. Instead of crowding fifty items onto a single page, designers should group similar products and decide when to utilize separate pages or distinct menu panels.

Crucially, the organization of these categories should be informed by the principles of menu engineering—a systematic, data-driven process that evaluates the popularity and profitability of each dish. The core metric here is the contribution margin, calculated by subtracting the food cost from the selling price. Items are subsequently plotted into a four-quadrant matrix.

Menu Engineering Category Popularity Profitability Strategic Action for Menu Design
Stars High High Highlight prominently in the most visible layout zones; utilize high-quality photography to protect and promote these items.
Plowhorses High Low Retain on the menu but demote visually; consider slight price adjustments or ingredient swaps to improve margins without utilizing premium design space.
Puzzles Low High Increase visibility through better placement (e.g., top right), stronger sensory descriptions, or appealing photography to drive sales.
Dogs Low Low Remove from the menu entirely to free up valuable visual real estate and simplify kitchen operations.

Historically, eye-tracking studies have suggested that diners follow a predictable scanning pattern on two-page printed menus, often looking first at the center of the right page, then to the top left, and finally to the top right—a concept known as the "Golden Triangle". By placing high-margin "Stars" and "Puzzles" in these prime visual zones, bakeries can naturally steer customers toward their most profitable offerings. For single-page menus, a standard Z-pattern scanning behavior is more common, dictating that highly profitable items should anchor the top corners of the layout.

3. How to Structure Each Product Listing

The anatomy of an individual product listing dictates how effectively the menu converts a browsing customer into a buyer. Each listing must contain specific elements arranged in a consistent, predictable hierarchy. Typically, this includes the product name, an optional image, a short description, flavor profiles, available sizes, serving information (e.g., "Serves 12-15"), and the starting or exact price. When applicable, customization notes and product identifiers or codes should be included to streamline the ordering process.

Descriptions must balance sensory appeal with extreme brevity. Long, rambling paragraphs dilute the visual impact of the menu and are rarely read in full. Conversely, overly simplistic labels fail to evoke an emotional response or justify premium pricing. Research into menu psychology indicates that specific, sensory language consistently outperforms generic labels in driving sales.

A weak description might read: "Chocolate Cake – Chocolate cake with chocolate frosting." This provides no tactile or qualitative information. An effective description elevates the product: "Midnight Chocolate Layer Cake – Three tiers of moist dark chocolate sponge filled with whipped Belgian chocolate ganache, finished with a smooth cocoa buttercream". This short, evocative sentence communicates texture, quality, and flavor expectations while remaining concise enough to fit neatly into a structured layout without disrupting the surrounding white space.

4. The Importance of Images in a Bakery Menu

In the confectionery industry, visual appetite appeal is a primary driver of consumer behavior. Food photography strongly influences how customers perceive the value, quality, and scale of bakery products. When deployed correctly, images communicate complex information faster than text, conveying the texture of a crumb, the gloss of a mirror glaze, the precision of piped decorations, and the generosity of a portion size.

Furthermore, imagery establishes brand personality and style. A rustic, moody photograph of a dusted sourdough loaf communicates a vastly different brand identity than a brightly lit, pastel-toned image of a modern fondant cake. High-quality, attractive images make a menu significantly easier to browse, serving as visual anchors that guide the customer's eye down the page.

However, images must support the menu layout rather than overwhelm it. A common error among novice designers is attempting to include a photograph for every single item on the menu. Photographing every product dilutes the visual impact, creates a chaotic, cluttered layout, and leaves insufficient space for essential typographic information. Instead, designers should use photography strategically, reserving images exclusively for high-margin "Stars," new seasonal products, or visually spectacular signature items that require physical demonstration to justify their price point.

5. How to Choose the Right Image for Each Product

Selecting the right product imagery requires a critical understanding of photographic composition, lighting, and brand consistency. A single, exceptionally strong image is always vastly superior to several mediocre, low-quality photos. Consistency is the hallmark of a professional bakery menu. Even small home bakeries can achieve a highly established, premium look by ensuring their menu photos share consistent lighting, similar backgrounds, harmonious image proportions, and a unified photography style.

When evaluating images for inclusion, the most recognizable and appealing part of the product must act as the focal point. Lighting plays a crucial role in texture rendering. For example, side lighting emphasizes the dimensional ridges of piped buttercream, while backlighting or side-back lighting highlights the translucent freshness of fruit toppings and the highly reflective shine of a ganache drip. Blurry, poorly lit, or low-resolution images actively damage the perceived quality of the food and must be strictly avoided.

Additionally, images heavily cluttered with excessive styling props—such as scattered flour, multiple utensils, and oversized floral arrangements—can distract the viewer from the product itself. The styling should provide subtle environmental context without competing for attention. Close-up images (macro photography) are highly useful for showcasing the intricate layers of a sliced cake or the delicate lamination of a pastry. Conversely, a wider composition is better suited for tiered celebration cakes or dessert tables where scale, vertical structure, and overall silhouette are the primary selling points.

6. Color Selection for Bakery Menus

Color choices fundamentally dictate the visual identity and perceived market positioning of a bakery brand. Rather than chasing fleeting design trends, a bakery's color palette must actively support its overarching brand narrative.

Different color palettes evoke specific psychological responses and set distinct expectations regarding the product line:

Palette Style Common Hues & Hex Codes Best Suited For Brand Perception
Neutral & Cream Warm white, beige, soft taupe, ivory Artisan bread, minimalist cafes, wedding cakes Elegant, organic, premium, understated.
Soft Pastel Light mint, powder blue, soft pink, lavender Cupcake shops, macarons, celebration cakes Friendly, sweet, approachable, nostalgic.
Warm Earthy Caramel, Bakery Brown (#BF8654), terracotta Artisanal bakeries, cookie shops, rustic pastries Cozy, comforting, homemade, rich.
Bold & Colorful Turquoise, coral, lemon yellow, vibrant pink Modern dessert brands, party treats, pop-up shops Energetic, playful, youthful, trend-forward.
Luxury / Dark Charcoal, navy, deep emerald with gold accents Chocolatiers, fine-dining desserts, patisseries Sophisticated, exclusive, high-end, dramatic.

When applying these colors to a menu layout, designers must carefully balance background tones, text colors, category labels, and prices. The primary goal is to avoid visual competition. If the background is overly vibrant, it will clash with the food photography.

Crucially, color selection must adhere strictly to accessibility standards regarding text readability. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) measure the contrast ratio between foreground text and background colors based on relative luminance. For a menu to remain highly legible to all customers, normal body text requires a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 (Level AA compliance), while large text (typically 18pt or larger, or 14pt bold) requires a minimum ratio of 3:1. Using the formula (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), designers can calculate these values, though automated tools are typically utilized. Pairing light gray text with a white background frequently fails these contrast requirements, resulting in a menu that is frustrating to read, especially on mobile devices under bright sunlight. Conversely, dark charcoal text on a warm cream background provides excellent mathematical contrast while maintaining a softer aesthetic than stark black and white.

7. Typography and Readability

Typography is where the aesthetic ambitions of a menu meet its functional requirements. The selection and arrangement of typefaces directly impact both the visual appeal and the usability of the document. A well-designed bakery menu relies on font hierarchy to guide the reader's eye seamlessly from the category heading, to the product name, down to the description, and finally to the price.

A standard and highly effective typographic strategy involves pairing contrasting font classifications, most commonly combining a characterful serif font with a clean sans-serif font. Serif fonts, characterized by the small decorative strokes at the ends of their letterforms, convey tradition, elegance, and warmth. Sans-serif fonts, lacking these strokes, offer a clean, modern, and highly legible appearance.

Typographic Element Recommended Font Style Example Font Pairing Strategy Purpose
Category Headings High-contrast Serif or Elegant Display Playfair Display, DM Serif Display, Lora Captures attention, establishes brand tone, anchors the section.
Product Names Medium-weight Serif or Bold Sans-Serif Merriweather, Montserrat, Work Sans Provides clear distinction and weight for individual items.
Product Descriptions Clean, readable Sans-Serif Open Sans, Inter, DM Sans, Source Sans 3 Ensures maximum legibility for smaller text blocks.
Prices Clean Sans-Serif (monospaced numbers) Barlow, Lato Allows prices to be easily scanned without stylistic distraction.

Highly decorative, script, or retro fonts (such as Verilet or Mayluna) can add a beautiful, bespoke feel to a bakery brand, but they must be used sparingly. While a flowing script font might look excellent for the bakery's logo or a large "Wedding Cakes" heading, it becomes entirely illegible when used for small product descriptions or pricing digits.

Readability is further influenced by font sizing and spacing. For printed menus, body text should generally not fall below 10-12pt. For digital menus viewed on screens, a baseline of 14-16px is strongly recommended to prevent eye strain. Adequate line height (typically around 120% to 140% of the font size) and generous letter spacing ensure that the text blocks do not look cramped or dense.

8. How to Avoid a Visually Confusing Menu

The most beautifully photographed cakes cannot save a menu that suffers from poor structural design. Visually confusing menus usually result from a lack of restraint. Common design pitfalls include deploying too many colors, utilizing more than two or three font families, and cluttering the page with excessive decorative elements like heavy borders, ornamental flourishes, and competing illustrations.

Another frequent error is allowing too many images to compete for attention on a single page, which entirely destroys the visual hierarchy. When every element on the page is equally prominent—when the descriptions are as bold as the headings, and the prices are boxed in heavy lines—the eye has no clear path to follow. This results in a crowded layout where the customer struggles to find basic information, increasing the cognitive load to unacceptable levels.

Visual hierarchy relies on the strategic use of white space (or negative space). White space is not merely empty space; it is an active design element that provides breathing room between categories, isolates important products, and groups related information together. By ensuring consistent image sizes, limiting descriptions to a few lines, clearly separating categories with ample spacing, and maintaining high textual contrast, designers can eliminate friction from the browsing experience.

9. Creating Space for Prices and Descriptions

A recurring challenge in bakery menu design is balancing the desire for a clean aesthetic with the necessity of displaying complex product information. Bakeries frequently offer items in multiple sizes (e.g., 6-inch, 8-inch, and 10-inch cakes), with various flavor variations, and specific customization notes. If the layout structure is not planned prior to adding the content, the designer will inevitably run out of space, resulting in tiny, unreadable fonts and crowded margins.

Designers must plan grid systems that leave generous room for product names, short descriptions, and versatile pricing structures. Depending on how the business sells its goods, prices can be displayed in several formats:

  • Single Prices: Used for standard, uniform items like individual cupcakes or standard dessert slices.
  • Starting Prices: (e.g., "From $45") Highly useful for custom cakes where the final price depends on the level of decoration and specific flavor fillings chosen.
  • Price Ranges: (e.g., "$30 - $55") Effective for items that vary by seasonal ingredient availability.
  • Multiple Size Prices: Displayed in columns next to the product name, allowing the customer to quickly compare the cost of different serving sizes.
  • Add-on Pricing: Clearly separated lists for premium fillings, custom toppers, or dietary modifications.

The presentation of pricing also intersects deeply with behavioral psychology. Menu engineering principles suggest that removing currency symbols (e.g., writing "45" instead of "$45.00") can subtly reduce the psychological friction and pain of spending. Furthermore, utilizing "charm pricing" (prices ending in .95 or .99) can make casual items feel like a better value, while round numbers signal premium quality in luxury bakeries.

Additionally, designers can employ "decoy pricing" and anchoring techniques to shift consumer behavior. Decoy pricing involves placing a highly expensive, premium tier item (an anchor) at the top of a category. For instance, a $150 elaborate tiered cake makes the mid-range $65 signature cakes—which often hold the highest profit margins—appear to be a much more reasonable value to the consumer by comparison.

10. Different Bakery Menu Styles

There is no single universally correct style for a bakery menu. The optimal visual direction depends entirely on the bakery's target audience, product positioning, and brand identity. Several dominant visual styles can be adapted depending on the business model:

Minimalist Bakery Menu

The minimalist style relies heavily on clean grid layouts, vast amounts of white or neutral space, highly restricted color palettes, and simple, modern typography. This style strips away all unnecessary decoration, allowing strong, high-quality product photography to take center stage. Minimalist menus are exceptionally easy to read and navigate. This visual direction is highly beneficial for modern artisan bakeries, sourdough specialists, and high-end dessert boutiques where the focus is entirely on the quality, purity, and craftsmanship of the ingredients.

Elegant / Luxury Bakery Menu

An elegant or luxury menu focuses on refined, high-contrast typography (frequently utilizing sophisticated serif fonts), neutral or dark color palettes with subtle metallic or muted accents, and carefully curated, moody imagery. Spacing is generous, and the overall presentation feels premium and exclusive. To prevent an elegant menu from becoming overly baroque or difficult to read, designers must exercise restraint with decorative details and ensure that script fonts are reserved strictly for large headings, never for prices or body copy. This style is ideal for premium wedding cake designers, fine chocolatiers, and high-end patisseries.

Colorful / Playful Bakery Menu

A playful menu utilizes brighter, more saturated color palettes, expressive and chunky typography, fun visual motifs, and highly energetic imagery. Category differentiation is often achieved through contrasting background colors or bold graphic shapes. While this style is vibrant and fun, maintaining structure is vital. The grid must remain organized so the burst of color does not devolve into visual chaos. This style perfectly suits custom birthday cake shops, modern cookie brands, donut shops, and businesses targeting a youthful, celebratory demographic.

11. Digital Bakery Menus

The transition from print to digital requires a fundamental shift in design logic. A digital menu is not simply a print menu viewed on a screen; it is an interactive interface. Digital menus are encountered on bakery websites, mobile screens, QR code landing pages, online ordering systems, and tablet displays inside the shop.

Mobile readability is the paramount concern, as the vast majority of digital menu browsing occurs on smartphones. A menu designed for a large, printed A4 or US Letter page should never simply be uploaded to a website as a static PDF or a tiny JPEG. When customers are forced to pinch and zoom continuously to read prices or descriptions on a phone, they experience friction that frequently leads to abandoned orders.

Digital menus must feature responsive layouts where text reflows to fit the width of the screen. Typography must be scaled using responsive CSS units (such as rem, em, or viewport width vw) to ensure legibility across all device sizes. Furthermore, digital environments allow for vertical scrolling, meaning the layout does not need to be artificially compressed to fit a single visual plane. Images must be properly optimized to ensure fast loading times without sacrificing resolution. Finally, digital menus benefit from clickable elements and clear calls to action, such as "Order Now" buttons placed strategically beneath high-margin items.

12. Bakery Menus for Social Media

Social media platforms like Instagram, Pinterest, and Facebook require businesses to adapt their menu information into highly visual, bite-sized formats. A comprehensive, multi-page menu rarely performs well as a standard social media post, as users scroll rapidly and have limited attention spans. Instead, the menu must be strategically deconstructed.

For social media, bakeries should utilize various specific formats:

  • Square or Vertical Posts (4:5): Ideal for highlighting a single "Star" product with a beautiful hero image, placing the description and price directly in the caption.
  • Carousel Posts: Excellent for displaying categories. Slide one acts as the category title (e.g., "Spring Tart Collection"), and subsequent slides feature individual product cards with an image, name, and short description.
  • Instagram Stories (9:16): Highly effective for limited-time offers, daily pastry availability, or sharing rapid, full-screen visual menus that followers can screenshot.
  • Highlight Covers: Permanent fixtures on an Instagram profile where full menus can be stored, categorized by product type (e.g., "Cakes," "Drinks," "Prices").

A social media menu prioritizes visual appeal and immediate engagement. Its primary function is not to provide exhaustive logistical details, but rather to capture attention, generate desire, and direct the customer to the full menu link in the bio or the designated ordering channel.

13. Bakery Menus for WhatsApp

In many international markets, WhatsApp serves as the primary engine for small business commerce, direct customer communication, and order processing. Adapting a menu specifically for WhatsApp requires understanding how files are shared and viewed within the messaging application.

Sending a customer an unnecessarily large number of separate images (e.g., ten different pictures of cakes with prices typed underneath) clutters their chat history, consumes their data, and makes it difficult for them to compare options. Instead, WhatsApp menus should be consolidated into practical formats:

  • Vertical Menu Images: A single, tall image designed specifically for mobile screens, containing clear, large text.
  • Multi-page PDF Menus: A lightweight PDF is highly professional, preserves high-quality text rendering without pixelation, and is easily saved and forwarded by customers.
  • WhatsApp Business Catalog: Utilizing the built-in catalog feature to create product cards with distinct images, descriptions, and pricing directly inside the app interface, allowing users to build a cart seamlessly.

Menus shared via WhatsApp must ensure that prices, product names, and step-by-step ordering instructions are impossible to miss, facilitating a fast and efficient transactional conversation without requiring the business owner to type out the same answers repeatedly.

14. Designing for Different Customer Journeys

A menu must be flexible enough to accommodate various customer intents. Consumers encounter a bakery menu at different stages of the purchasing journey, and the design must cater to these varying psychological states.

Some customers arrive with a specific utility in mind—they already know what they want. They need a vanilla celebration cake for a birthday party this weekend, and they are aggressively scanning for prices, sizes, and availability. For this customer, the menu structure must be highly organized, logically categorized, and effortlessly scannable, allowing them to extract data instantly.

Conversely, other customers are merely browsing for inspiration. They may have discovered the bakery through an aesthetically pleasing image on Pinterest, Google, or Instagram and are casually exploring the offerings. For these customers, the menu must provide visual inspiration through high-quality photography, enticing descriptions, and clear brand storytelling. By utilizing strong category headings (for the specific searcher) and strategically placed hero images (for the inspired browser), a well-designed menu accommodates both consumer behaviors simultaneously.

15. Brand Consistency

A bakery menu does not exist in a vacuum; it is a vital component of the business's broader visual ecosystem. The menu must connect visually and thematically with the bakery's logo, physical storefront, packaging, website, and social media presence.

Consistency is achieved by rigorously applying the same color palettes, typography rules, and photographic styles across all mediums. If a bakery uses a playful, bright pink box with a retro font for its packaging, but hands the customer a stark, minimalist, black-and-white menu printed on standard copy paper, the cognitive disconnect damages the brand's perceived value. Consistent visual branding builds trust, signaling to the consumer that the business is established, professional, and attentive to detail—qualities that directly translate to their expectations of the food quality.

16. A Practical Bakery Menu Design Workflow

Creating a professional menu is a systematic process. Following a structured workflow prevents designers and business owners from becoming overwhelmed by layout decisions before the content is properly formulated.

  1. Define the Menu's Purpose: Determine exactly where this specific menu will be used (e.g., printed for the counter, uploaded as a digital PDF, or formatted for Instagram carousels).
  2. List and Cost All Products: Compile every item to be sold. Crucially, ensure exact ingredient costs are calculated to determine the contribution margin for each item, informing where they should sit on the menu.
  3. Group Products into Categories: Organize the list into logical, customer-friendly sections (e.g., "Cupcakes," "Celebration Cakes").
  4. Define Necessary Information: Decide exactly what data (size, price, flavor, dietary notes) each product requires to avoid mid-design text overload.
  5. Select Product Images: Choose high-quality, consistently lit photographs for high-margin "Stars" and "Puzzles".
  6. Establish the Visual Style: Choose a direction (minimalist, luxury, playful) that perfectly aligns with the brand's core positioning.
  7. Select Colors and Typography: Define the brand's palette, rigorously ensuring WCAG contrast compliance (4.5:1 ratio), and select readable serif and sans-serif font pairings.
  8. Create the Layout Grid: Build the wireframe, placing categories logically (utilizing the Golden Triangle or Z-pattern) and leaving generous white space.
  9. Insert Content: Add the product names, brief sensory-rich descriptions, and strategic pricing.
  10. Evaluate Hierarchy and Spacing: Ensure the eye moves smoothly across the page and that prices are anchored correctly.
  11. Test for Mobile Readability: If the menu is digital, view the file on a smartphone to ensure absolutely no zooming is required to read body text.
  12. Check for Consistency: Ensure the final output matches the logo, packaging, and social media style.
  13. Export and Publish: Save the file in the appropriate format (high-resolution PDF for print, optimized responsive HTML or compressed PDF/JPEG for digital).
  14. Review and Update: Schedule regular reviews (e.g., quarterly) to adjust pricing dynamically based on fluctuating ingredient costs and updated sales data.

17. Common Bakery Menu Design Mistakes

To ensure a highly functional end product, designers and bakery owners should actively avoid these frequent errors, which consistently degrade the customer experience:

Common Mistake Negative Impact Strategic Correction
Inconsistent Photography Destroys the professional aesthetic; makes the brand look amateur and disorganized. Use consistent lighting, similar backgrounds, and identical aspect ratios for all featured products.
Lack of Visual Hierarchy Causes visual confusion; the customer doesn't know where to look. Use varied font sizes, weights, and intentional white space to guide the eye intuitively.
Hiding or Obscuring Prices Creates frustration and distrust; slows down the ordering process. Ensure pricing is clearly legible, even if currency symbols are strategically removed.
Overly Long Descriptions Clutters the layout and causes readers to skip the text entirely. Use brief, sensory-rich phrases focused solely on flavor and texture.
Too Many Fonts Creates a chaotic, unprofessional, and difficult-to-read document. Limit the design to two complementary font families (e.g., one serif, one sans-serif).
Low Text Contrast Renders the menu unreadable for many users, violating WCAG accessibility guidelines. Ensure text meets a minimum 4.5:1 contrast ratio against its background.
Ignoring Mobile Users Leads to cart abandonment as users struggle with tiny text on small smartphone screens. Design digital menus specifically for vertical scrolling and responsive viewports.
Static / Un-updatable Files Causes the business to lose money when ingredient costs rise but the menu cannot be easily changed. Build the menu in a system or template that allows for rapid, frictionless price updates.
Overloading a Single Page Induces decision fatigue and makes the menu feel crowded and cheap. Group similar products and use multiple pages or sections if the catalog is large.

19. Final Bakery Menu Design Checklist

Before sending a menu to the printer or uploading it to a digital platform, operators should rigorously evaluate the layout against a comprehensive pre-publication checklist:

  • Organization: Are the products divided into logical, customer-friendly categories based on how they shop?
  • Product Information: Are flavors, serving sizes, variations, and customization limits clearly stated?
  • Images: Are the photographs high quality, consistently lit, and focused strictly on high-margin items?
  • Colors & Contrast: Does the text meet WCAG contrast accessibility standards (minimum 4.5:1) against the background?
  • Typography: Are the fonts legible at small sizes? Are decorative fonts restricted to headings only?
  • Spacing: Is there sufficient white space around categories to prevent visual fatigue and clutter?
  • Prices: Are prices accurate, easy to locate, and formatted to reduce price sensitivity (e.g., charm pricing, removed symbols)?
  • Mobile Usability: Can the digital version be read effortlessly on a smartphone screen without pinching or zooming?
  • Branding: Does the menu visually match the bakery’s packaging, website, and social media presence?
  • Ordering Instructions: Are contact details, advance notice requirements, and payment methods clearly visible?
  • Social Media Compatibility: Can the menu be easily broken down into readable slides for Instagram or Facebook?
  • WhatsApp Usability: Is the file size optimized for quick downloading and forwarding in messaging apps?
  • Overall Visual Clarity: Does the layout guide the customer smoothly from category, to product, to price?
